<BigPicture title="A name is a hint, not a health certificate">

[Networking for system design](/learn/networking-for-system-design) already traced one request through DNS, TCP, TLS, and a load balancer. This lesson is the **name and membership** problem: who is allowed to receive the next TokenX debit or Ad Click ingest packet, how long a dead address can stay cached, and what happens when every cache expires at once.

DNS answers, OS caches, recursive resolvers, browser caches, CoreDNS, Consul-style catalogs, and Kubernetes Endpoints are **derived routing state**. They can be rebuilt. They are not the TokenX ledger, not the billed-click log, and not proof that a process can still commit.

Authoritative for *where work may go* is the **service registry / control plane** plus the instance’s own **health** (and, for money, the ledger’s ability to accept a unique `(tenant, transfer_id)`). Authoritative for *whether money moved* remains the ledger. A 60-second TTL is a freshness *budget* for a photocopy of an address set, not a failover SLO.

</BigPicture>

<UnderTheHood title="Stacked caches, then a registry that actually forgets the dead">

### The resolver chain is several independent TTLs

A lookup is not one round trip. Typical path (illustrative):

1. Application or language runtime cache.
2. OS stub resolver (`nscd`, systemd-resolved, glibc).
3. Recursive resolver (VPC DNS, 8.8.8.8, corporate).
4. Authoritative nameserver for the zone.

Each layer may honor, clamp, or ignore TTL. **Negative caching** (NXDOMAIN, empty NOERROR) often uses SOA `minimum` / RFC 2308 and can outlive the operator’s mental “we deleted the record.” Split-horizon answers differ by client network: a laptop VPN and a cluster node can receive different A/AAAA sets for the same name.

SRV and CNAME add extra lookups. An A/AAAA set is what the client finally dials. SRV is useful for port discovery; it does not make the target healthy.

<Calculation label="Stale window after a collector dies (illustrative)">

Ad Click anycast name `ingest.ads.example` has TTL **60 s** at the authoritative server. Recursive resolvers clamp to 60 s. A collector process also caches the resolved set for **30 s**. Worst-case time a client keeps sending to a dead POP after the registry removed it: about **60 s + 30 s = 90 s**, plus the TCP timeout if the address still SYN-ACKs on a blackholed VIP.

TokenX internal name `ledger-api.svc` with TTL **5 s** and no extra app cache: stale window ≈ **5 s**, which is still long enough to send several 200 ms debits at a draining pod. DNS TTL is not [load-balancing drain](/learn/load-balancing-deep-dive). Drain waits for in-flight commits; TTL waits for photocopies to expire.

If 8,000 clients share a 60 s TTL and all expire together, the thundering herd is **8,000** simultaneous queries plus reconnects, not one polite refresh.

</Calculation>

### DNS versus a registry that can forget

- **DNS-only discovery:** cheap, universal, cached. Failover is **TTL-bound**. Health is whoever still answers the name. Weighted records and anycast are routing tricks, not instance health.
- **Client-side discovery:** the client watches a catalog (Consul, etcd, Eureka-class) and picks a healthy instance. Update latency is watch/index time, often seconds or less, but the catalog is another control plane to operate. The client now owns retry and outlier policy.
- **Kubernetes Endpoints / EndpointSlice + CoreDNS:** kube-proxy or an endpoint controller is the membership owner. CoreDNS is a **derived** A/AAAA view of ready pods. A pod can be Ready in DNS while its ledger pool is exhausted. Readiness must include the dependency that matters, or TokenX will debit-route to a process that can only 503.
- **Sidecar / mesh endpoint discovery:** xDS is another derived view. Policy lives in [Service mesh and proxies](/learn/service-mesh-and-proxies); this lesson only requires that empty endpoint sets **fail closed** for money.

Registration, heartbeat, and **deregistration** are the actual failover path. A crash without deregistration leaves a zombie until TTL or the lease expires. A graceful stop that removes the endpoint *and* drains is the combination that [load balancing](/learn/load-balancing-deep-dive) already demanded.

<InteractiveCalculation mode="little-law" title="Reconnect herd is extra in-flight work at the resolver and the VIP" />

Little’s Law at the resolver: if 2,000 TokenX clients all refresh every 5 s, mean lookup rate is 400 queries/s. After a zone change that invalidates everyone, the spike is closer to 2,000 queries in one RTT. That is offered load on CoreDNS, not proof the ledger is up.

<MentalModel title="A phone book with yesterday’s numbers">

DNS is a printed phone book. The registry is the live switchboard. Calling a number still in the book after the desk moved does not transfer money; it rings an empty room. Reprint delay is TTL. The switchboard forgetting a dead desk is deregistration. TokenX does not leave a voicemail that says “paid.”

Retain: **caches are derived, health and registry are the routing authority, money still commits only in the ledger, and failover delay is the max of stacked TTLs plus connect timeout—not the time you edited the record.**

</MentalModel>

**Authoritative routing state:** the registry (or Kubernetes endpoint controller) and instance readiness that includes the ledger or collector dependency. **Authoritative money / billed facts:** TokenX ledger plus unique `(tenant, transfer_id)`; Ad Click log plus `click_id`. **Derived:** stub, recursive, and application DNS caches; CoreDNS A records; VIP membership; anycast announcements. Solid edges are a successful resolve-then-commit path. Dashed edges are stale TTL to a dead pod, empty-set fail-closed, and connect failure that must re-resolve rather than reuse the photocopy. Recovery is deregister, drain, expire or bypass TTL on hard failure, and never treat a cached address as a commit.

<RealExamples title="TokenX empty-set 503; Ad Click stale anycast">

**TokenX.** `ledger-api` is a Kubernetes Service. Readiness requires a successful unique-id insert probe against the ledger, not merely “process listens on 8443.” If the Ready set is empty, kube-proxy has nowhere legal to send SYN. The API returns **503** and does **not** ACK a debit. Clients retry the **same** `transfer_id` after jitter. They do not pin a 10-minute application cache of pod IPs to “keep availability.”

**Ad Click.** Public ingest uses an anycast name with TTL 30 s in front of regional collectors. If one POP dies, BGP withdraw is faster than DNS, but clients that resolved a unicast fallback name still hammer the dead POP until TTL. Ingest **fail-opens** to remaining healthy collectors. Billed identity is still `click_id` on the durable log. A stale DNS answer is lost availability at one POP, not permission to ACK from memory or drop uniqueness. Dashboards that lag because a POP vanished are **derived**.

<InterviewTraps title="Discovery claims that fail a staff+ interview">

- **“We updated DNS, so failover is instant.”** Stacked caches and negative TTL disagree. Bound the window.
- **“Kubernetes DNS is health.”** CoreDNS is a derived view of Ready. Ready that ignores the ledger is a lie.
- **“Client-side discovery is always better than DNS.”** You bought a catalog, watches, and client retry policy. Name the control plane.
- **“Last-known-good keeps payments up.”** That is fail-open routing. TokenX money fail-closes on empty discovery.
- **“SRV records give you service mesh.”** SRV gives port names. Mesh policy is a different lesson.
- **“Anycast plus DNS means Ad Click cannot double-bill.”** Anycast is a routing hint. Uniqueness is `click_id` on the log.

</InterviewTraps>

<Tradeoffs title="Freshness, query load, and who owns membership">

- **Short TTL versus resolver load:** 5 s money names cut stale windows and create herds. 60 s public names spare recursive resolvers and slow failover.
- **DNS-only versus registry watch:** DNS is dumb and global; watches are fast and operationally heavy.
- **Deep Ready versus shallow listen:** Deep probes eject pods during a shared ledger outage and can empty the VIP—the same trap as [load balancing](/learn/load-balancing-deep-dive). Shallow probes route to processes that cannot commit. For TokenX, empty-and-503 is better than 200-from-nowhere.
- **App-level cache versus OS cache:** extra cache layers extend the stale bound you must quote in an interview.

Correctness of spendable routing is not a TTL knob. Completeness of ads ingest during a dead POP is an availability knob with uniqueness still at the log.

</Tradeoffs>

<InterviewAnswer>

“I treat DNS as a stacked cache of routing photocopies. Failover delay is the max of application cache, stub, recursive, and TTL, plus connect timeout—not the time I edited the record. Negative cache can hide a restored name. The registry or Kubernetes endpoint controller plus honest Ready is membership; CoreDNS is derived. TokenX fail-closes if the ready set is empty: 503, same transfer_id on retry, no last-known-good VIP. Ad Click may fail-open ingest to remaining collectors while click_id uniqueness stays on the billed log. I do not call a name lookup health, linearizable failover, or a commit.”

</InterviewAnswer>
