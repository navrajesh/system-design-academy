<BigPicture title="A timeout is suspicion, not a death certificate">

Failure detectors in production systems are almost always **timeouts plus gossip**: a heartbeat missed for `session.timeout.ms`, a replica that has not fetched within `replica.lag.time.max.ms`, a lease the lock service will not renew. None of those signals is ground truth. A 40-second garbage-collection pause, a brief network blip, or a stuck NIC looks identical to a crashed process until later evidence arrives. Acting on that suspicion too quickly **falsely declares death** and starts a replacement. Waiting too long **misses a zombie** that still believes it owns the shard and is still writing.

Replacement therefore creates a **dual-owner window**. The detector may have already given the partition, lock, or group assignment to a successor while the previous holder is paused, partitioned, or finishing an in-flight call. Exclusion is not the timeout. Exclusion is a **fencing token, leader epoch, or generation checked at the resource**, after that high-water value is **durable**. Lease expiry at the coordinator does not notify the ledger. See [distributed locks and leases](/learn/distributed-locks-and-leases).

This lesson is about cluster and process failure detection and the recovery that follows: broker death, controller death, consumer death, catch-up, high-watermark as a read gate, and leader-epoch truncation. It does not re-derive the consumer-group protocol; two clocks and the dual-owner window already live in [consumer groups and rebalancing](/learn/consumer-groups-and-rebalancing). Partition logs, ISR, and controller-versus-partition election live in [Kafka internals](/learn/kafka-internals). Promotion without two writers is the same shape as [replication and failover](/learn/replication-and-failover).

TokenX settlement cannot treat group assignment as a mutex: a worker can die mid-ledger write and a successor can replay the same event. Ad Click collectors can die with lag that only leftover consume rate can drain. Neither system gets **safe failover** from a health check. Name the fence and name that the high-water token survives restart.

</BigPicture>

<UnderTheHood title="Suspect, elect, fence, then catch up">

### Suspicion is a timer, with two error modes

A detector that requires a heartbeat every `H` and declares death after `T` has two failure modes you must name in an interview:

- **False positive:** the process is alive but silent (stop-the-world pause, packet loss, scheduler starvation). The system elects a successor. Availability of *that* owner drops; correctness is at risk until fencing holds.
- **False negative:** the process still **heartbeats** while work is wedged (`max.poll.interval.ms` vs session), or `T` is so long that a crash is not declared. The detector thinks it is healthy. A process **partitioned from the detector but still writing the resource** is the other error: the detector will eventually **suspect** (false-positive crash declaration) while the zombie keeps writing—split brain, not a missed heartbeat.

You cannot drive both error rates to zero with one timeout. Short `T` recovers faster and misfires more. Long `T` hides rolling restarts and extends the zombie-hold window. Size `T` as an operational SLO against measured pause and network tails, then assume it will still be wrong sometimes.

Kafka uses several independent timers. They are not interchangeable:

| Detector | What it watches | Who acts | What it does **not** prove |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Broker session to the metadata quorum | Broker process liveness | Controller | Disk health, ISR catch-up, or fsync |
| `replica.lag.time.max.ms` | Follower fetch progress | Partition leader | That the lagging replica is dead |
| Consumer `session.timeout.ms` / heartbeats | Member liveness to the **group coordinator** | Coordinator | That the processing loop is making progress |
| `max.poll.interval.ms` | Poll-loop liveness | Coordinator | Exclusive sink ownership |
| Lock/lease TTL | Recorded ownership at the lock service | Lock service | That the store has observed a new epoch |

Heartbeats and poll interval as *two consumer clocks* are already specified in the [consumer-groups lesson](/learn/consumer-groups-and-rebalancing). Here the point is cluster-wide: the same heartbeat-versus-work split exists for brokers (session up, replica fetch stalled) and for workers (lease renewed, ledger call hung).

### Lease expiry is reassignment, not exclusion

A lease or session timeout lets the **authority** grant the same resource to someone else. It does not retract bytes already on the wire, cancel a JDBC commit, or update the sink. Until the new owner **durably retains** a higher fencing token at the resource, and the resource **atomically compares** that token on every mutation, the old holder can still succeed.

That is the same protocol as the locks lesson: retain-then-work, reload the high-water token after restart, reject lower epochs. Kafka's **leader epoch** is that token on the replica path. A consumer **generation** is that token only at the group coordinator (membership and offset commits). It is **not** a sink fence unless the ledger stores and checks it. Prefer a unique `event_id` in the same database transaction when the sink does not speak Kafka generations.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not say safe failover">

Failover is safe only if you can name (1) who increments the epoch, (2) which resource rejects the old epoch, and (3) that the high-water token is durable across that resource's restart. A load-balancer health check, a DNS cutover, or a coordinator reassignment is none of those three.

</Callout>

### Split brain is two owners of one log or one sink

**Split brain** means two processes both believe they may write the same partition, shard, or ledger key. Detection timeouts cause it; fencing contains it.

- **Kafka partition leadership.** One metadata/controller authority (KRaft or ZooKeeper as implementation) assigns the leader and the epoch. Followers and clients must not invent a second leader map. A **zombie leader** that was isolated and then heals still has its old log and old epoch. Replicas that have learned epoch 9 **reject** epoch-8 produce and replica fetch. That is fencing at the log copies, the same idea as a store rejecting token 42 after retaining 43.
- **Consumer generation.** The coordinator is authoritative for **membership**, not for ledger balances. Generation 13 proves the successor may fetch. It does not stop generation 12's in-flight `UPDATE`. Unless the sink checks generation or a unique event id, you have split brain at the **effect**, which is the usual TokenX bug.
- **Controller versus partition.** Two controllers would be metadata split brain; the metadata quorum's terms exist to prevent that. A controller restart is not partition split brain. Existing partition leaders keep serving on the last assignment. See internals: controller failover ≠ partition reassignment.

### Three Kafka deaths, three recoveries

**Broker failure (data-plane leader or follower).** The controller removes the broker from membership when its metadata session expires. Partitions that had a leader on that broker get a **new leader from the current ISR**, with a new **leader epoch**. Followers catch up by replica fetch. The recovering former leader, if its log diverged, truncates a suffix using **leader-epoch end offsets** (KIP-101): it asks the current leader for the last offset of each epoch and cuts disagreeing local bytes. That is **not** “the new leader rewinds to the old high-watermark.” High-watermark remains the **consumer read gate**: ordinary consumers fetch offsets strictly below HW. Offsets the old leader held only locally sat above HW and must not have been consumed. Unclean election (a non-ISR replica) can still drop acknowledged offsets; keep it off unless you are explicitly accepting that delivery envelope.

**Controller failure.** Metadata writes pause until a new controller loads the metadata log. Preferred-leader elections and ISR updates that need the controller wait. **Partition leaders already assigned keep serving.** Clients that hit a fenced or moved leader receive `NOT_LEADER_OR_FOLLOWER` or `FENCED_LEADER_EPOCH` and refresh metadata. Do not treat controller election as cluster-wide failover of every topic.

**Consumer / worker failure.** The group coordinator (a broker) expires the member on session timeout or poll-interval expiry and starts a new generation. Recovery is: successor fetches from the last **committed offset** (group checkpoint, not sink proof), dual-owner window at the sink, catch-up of lag. Static membership delays that detection until the session timeout even if the process is a corpse—already covered as a group-protocol trade-off; here it is the false-negative detector for “host down, assignment still granted.”

### Catch-up needs leftover rate, with produce still running

Lag after death is not “how long the process was down.” It is backlog size divided by **leftover** copy or apply rate while new records still arrive.

<Calculation label="Ad Click collector death: leftover consume rate">

Assumptions (illustrative, not a measured cluster): partition 4 receives 12,000 clicks/s at 400 B each (4.8 MB/s into the leader before protocol overhead). Collector C4 dies. Suspicion takes session.timeout.ms = 45 s, then assignment moves. Applied offset is 3,600,000 behind the high-watermark when the successor starts (45 s of missed apply at 12,000/s = 540,000, plus 3,060,000 already queued—planning mix).
If the successor applies 18,000 records/s and produce continues at 12,000 records/s, leftover catch-up rate is 6,000 records/s.
Catch-up time: 3,600,000 / 6,000 = 600 s (10 min), ignoring rebalance pause, GC, and sink contention.
If apply equals produce, lag never drains. If apply is 10,000/s, lag grows at 2,000/s without bound.
Broker-side analogue: a follower **out of ISR** for 90 s at 8 MB/s leader produce accumulates 720 MB of missed bytes. Fetch at 20 MB/s leaves 12 MB/s leftover; 720 / 12 = 60 s to **rejoin the ISR** and become a durability witness again. HW did **not** stall on that corpse: remaining ISR members already defined the consumer-visible prefix.

</Calculation>

ISR rejoin is the replica version of leftover-rate math. Kafka HW is the exclusive prefix of the **current** ISR. When a follower leaves ISR, remaining members define HW; consumers do not wait for the dead replica. A lagging replica **still in ISR** does delay HW until it catches up or is dropped. Consumers wait for HW (replica coverage), not for a dead collector's apply loop.

</UnderTheHood>

<MentalModel title="Smoke alarm, hotel keycard, and the photocopier roster">

A smoke alarm is a detector, not an arson report. It can scream because someone burnt toast (false positive) or stay quiet while a wiring fire smolders (false negative). Evacuating the building does not confiscate the old guest's keycard. The **door** checks the newest encoded generation; checkout time at the desk does not.

Kafka's registrar (controller) names the clerk for each ledger (partition leader) and stamps an epoch on the appointment. Copyists (ISR) refuse pages stamped with an old epoch. A separate coat-check lead (group coordinator) hands attendants exclusive rails for this shift number (generation). The closet (sink) still needs the claim check—unique event id or a stored token—because an attendant can be mid-hang when the shift changes.

The “safe to read” stamp on the ledger is how far every on-duty copyist has photocopied (high-watermark), not how far the last clerk's pen reached (log-end offset). When a clerk is replaced, the new clerk usually keeps the pages they already copied. The deposed clerk cuts pages that disagree with the epoch map; nobody throws away the whole book back to the last public stamp.

Retain: **timeouts suspect; epochs fence; HW gates reads; catch-up is leftover rate; membership, log, and sink are three authorities.**

</MentalModel>

The **metadata controller** assigns partition leaders and **leader epochs**; the log does not elect the epoch. The **partition log** is authoritative for record bytes until retention. Replica fetch is **asynchronous** from that log into the current ISR. **High-watermark** is the exclusive consumer **read gate** derived from current ISR coverage—it is not an input to consumer generation. When a follower leaves ISR, remaining members define HW. The **group coordinator** assigns generation 12 then 13; that is membership, not a ledger mutex. The **ledger plus unique event ids** is the default TokenX fence. Dashed edges are replica fetch, stale produce, delayed apply, and epoch reject. Timeout suspicion is not a fence.

<RealExamples title="TokenX dual owners and Ad Click lag after death">

**TokenX settlement worker dies mid-ledger write.** Worker `settlement-2` has an **in-flight** `BEGIN` for event `S-991` that already left the JVM when the process is killed. Heartbeats stop. After `session.timeout.ms` the coordinator creates generation 13 and assigns partition 3 to `settlement-5`. `settlement-5` fetches `S-991` again because the Kafka offset was not committed. If that in-flight commit also lands, two processes owned the same ledger key during the window. **Group assignment is not a mutex.** Safety is `UNIQUE(event_id)` in the same transaction as the balance mutation, then the offset commit. A stored `kafka_generation` is optional and only a fence if the ledger compares it after a durable retain. Same shape as [locks and leases](/learn/distributed-locks-and-leases).

A **partition leader** for the settlement topic dying is a different death: ISR election, epoch bump, replica reject of the old leader, catch-up. Do not page that as a consumer-group incident, and do not “fail over” by pointing producers at a follower.

**Ad Click collector death.** Collectors in `group.id=adclick-ingest` own click partitions. When collector C4 dies, suspicion waits for the session timeout; lag versus high-watermark grows at the produce rate the whole time. After assignment, catch-up needs leftover apply rate as in the calculation: 3,600,000 records at 6,000 leftover records/s is ten minutes of stale dashboards if the aggregator reads applied state. Page on **partition lag**, not on member count. Adding collectors beyond the partition count does not drain C4's backlog. Replica catch-up after a **broker** leaves that topic's ISR is independent: remaining ISR members keep HW moving; the recovering follower uses leftover fetch rate to rejoin as a witness, not to unblock consumers who were waiting on a corpse. Aggregates remain derived; rebuild from the retained click log if the projection diverged during dual apply.

<InterviewTraps title="Detection slogans that fail in staff+ interviews">

- **“The node is dead; the heartbeat timed out.”** You have suspicion. Ask about pause budgets, network blips, and what the zombie can still touch.
- **“Lease expiry immediately excludes the old holder.”** Expiry reassigns at the lock or coordinator. The resource excludes a stale token only after it has durably observed a higher epoch. Cross-link [locks](/learn/distributed-locks-and-leases).
- **“The new consumer generation fences the database.”** Generation fences offset commits at the coordinator. The sink must check a token or unique event id.
- **“Controller failover is cluster failover.”** Metadata authority moves. Partition leaders usually keep serving. Fence and reassignment happen on **partition** leadership change.
- **“After failover the new leader truncates to the old HW.”** HW is why consumers must not have read that suffix. The new ISR leader typically keeps its log; diverged followers truncate by **leader epoch**.
- **“We fail over safely because the load balancer removed the old IP.”** DNS and L4 health checks are not fencing. Two writers need an epoch at storage.
- **“ISR election means no data loss.”** ISR-only election plus `acks=all` and `min.insync.replicas` is the delivery envelope. Unclean election, page-cache power loss of the whole ISR, and retention expiry still lose prefixes. See [delivery](/learn/kafka-delivery-and-failure) and [internals](/learn/kafka-internals).
- **“Lag will catch up when the collector restarts.”** Only if leftover apply or fetch rate is positive. Equals produce → flat. Below produce → unbounded growth.
- **“Safe failover.”** Refuse the phrase unless you name the fence and the durable high-water token.

Follow-up you should invite: “Where is the dual-owner window, and which store rejects the stale write after a crash-restart of that store?”

</InterviewTraps>

<Tradeoffs title="False positives, zombie time, and how hard you fence">

- **Short versus long suspicion timeouts:** short `session.timeout.ms` / replica-lag bounds recover faster and create more false-positive elections and dual-owner windows; long timeouts hide deploys and leave corpses assigned.
- **ISR-only versus unclean leader election:** ISR-only preserves the acknowledged prefix at the cost of unavailability when no ISR member remains; unclean restores a leader and can drop acked offsets. That is a delivery choice, not a detection tweak.
- **Epoch fencing at replicas versus event-id idempotency at the sink:** replica epochs stop split-brain produces; they do not serialize ledger effects. Event ids (or a stored generation) close consumer overlap. You usually need both layers.
- **Volatile versus durable high-water tokens:** in-memory max epoch is fast and loses exclusion across resource restart. Durable retain-before-work is the safety boundary.
- **Controller-independent data plane:** partition leaders surviving controller death preserves availability of reads and writes on the last map, at the cost of frozen ISR and assignment changes until metadata returns.
- **Catch-up capacity versus cost:** leftover consume or replica-fetch rate is extra CPU, disk, and sink capacity you pay for even when healthy, or you accept multi-minute lag after every death.

Correctness is fencing plus idempotency. Timeouts only trade how quickly you *start* the unsafe window.

</Tradeoffs>
