<BigPicture title="CAP describes behavior during a partition">

When nodes cannot communicate, each operation needs an explicit partition policy. A balance debit may reject or wait on a side that cannot prove quorum, while a cached portfolio read may remain available and return labeled stale data. Describing the whole product as “CP” or “AP” hides these operation-level decisions.

It is not a permanent selection of two letters, and “consistency” in CAP means linearizable behavior—not database constraint validity.

</BigPicture>

<UnderTheHood title="Quorums intersect only under assumptions">

With N replicas, a common rule is <code>W + R &gt; N</code>, so a read quorum overlaps the last write quorum. This does not automatically guarantee linearizability: sloppy quorums, concurrent versions, clock-based conflict resolution, and failed read repair can change behavior. A linearizable leader design also needs one active epoch, fencing against stale leaders, and a read protocol that proves the leader still holds authority.

Leader-based systems route writes through one elected leader. In a **three-replica** group, majority is **2-of-3**: the leader plus **either** follower, not all three copies. During a partition, only a side that can form that majority may retain or elect a leader for coordinated writes.

The **isolated minority** cannot commit linearizable writes. It may still serve explicitly stale, labeled reads if product policy allows. **Epoch fencing** rejects minority writes that carry an older epoch, even if that replica still believes it is leader.

**Quorum availability is not full-cluster availability.** A majority can keep committing while one replica is down. The group still has no majority—and cannot commit linearizable writes—if two of three replicas are lost, or if a partition splits **1/1/1**. Even a live majority can be unavailable during election or when the operation deadline expires.

The policy can differ by operation:

- **TokenX debit:** require a fenced leader and quorum commit; reject when authority cannot be proven.
- **Portfolio read:** return the newest locally known version with an “as of” timestamp if product policy permits staleness.
- **User preference update:** accept concurrent versions and merge only if the field-level conflict rule is deterministic and user-safe.

</UnderTheHood>

<MentalModel title="Begin with the invariant, not CAP">

Ask what must never happen. A username may be briefly unavailable rather than assigned twice. A social feed can show posts late. A TokenX wallet must not spend the same balance twice.

Think of two bank branches after their phone line breaks. “Keep the bank available” is too vague: both branches may show yesterday's statement, but both must not authorize spending the same final $100. Decide separately what each operation may do while coordination is unavailable.

Then define operation-level guarantees: read-your-writes, monotonic reads, causal order, bounded staleness, or linearizability.

</MentalModel>

The group has **three replicas**: leader, follower A, and follower B. Authoritative TokenX transfer order lives in the replicated log after a **2-of-3** commit: the leader plus **either** follower. In this partition that pair is leader + A; B is the isolated minority, not a fourth copy. The same debit could have committed with leader + B if A had been the isolated one. Waiting for all three acks is a stricter durability choice, not the majority rule.

The isolated minority cannot commit linearizable writes. Product policy may still let it answer a portfolio query if the response exposes version and staleness. Epoch 52 fences epoch-51 writes. Majority-side writes can continue while B is unreachable. They cannot continue under a **1/1/1** split or after losing two of three replicas, because no majority remains. Quorum availability is not full-cluster availability. Consistency and availability are chosen per operation, not per product logo.

<RealExamples title="Feeds, inventory, and TokenX">

A feed read can usually remain available under partition and reconcile later, while privacy-list updates may require stricter coordination. Checkout inventory often uses reservations to bound oversell while allowing catalog reads to remain available. TokenX transfer authorization requires strongly coordinated balance or reservation state, while portfolio charts can be eventually consistent.

<InterviewTraps title="Consistency shortcuts to reject">

- Saying a system “chooses AP” or “chooses CP” without naming the operation and partition behavior.
- Assuming quorum arithmetic alone makes reads linearizable.
- Using last-write-wins with unsynchronized wall clocks for financial state.
- Demanding strong consistency for every derived view.
- Forgetting client session guarantees after failover.
- Drawing three replica arrows as if every write needed all three copies. In RF=3, majority is 2-of-3: leader plus either follower.
- Treating “a quorum is up” as “the cluster is fully available.” The isolated replica is still down for its clients; a 1/1/1 split or two-of-three loss has no majority at all.

</InterviewTraps>

<Tradeoffs title="Coordination buys invariants with latency and availability">

Linearizable operations require coordination and can become unavailable without a quorum or within a deadline. Eventual consistency keeps selected operations available but pushes conflict semantics into the application. Causal consistency preserves meaningful order with less coordination than total order, at extra metadata cost.

Waiting for all three replicas on every TokenX debit buys extra copy durability at the cost of availability: a single follower outage would stall commits even though a 2-of-3 majority still exists. Majority commit keeps writes available through one replica loss and still cannot commit after two losses or a 1/1/1 split.

Strong coordination is inappropriate for derived views whose value decays slowly and whose unavailability would hurt users more than bounded staleness. Eventual conflict resolution is inappropriate when two accepted operations can jointly violate a non-mergeable invariant such as uniqueness or conserved balance.

</Tradeoffs>

<InterviewAnswer>

“I avoid labeling an entire product CP or AP. CAP becomes relevant when replicas cannot communicate, so I classify operations by invariant. Token transfer authorization requires a fenced leader and a 2-of-3 majority on three replicas—the leader plus either follower—not all three copies. The isolated minority cannot commit that debit; epoch fencing rejects its writes, and it may still serve a labeled stale portfolio if product policy allows. Quorum availability is not full-cluster availability: the majority can write while one replica is down, but a 1/1/1 split or two-of-three loss has no majority. I then state membership, commit, read, failover, conflict, and client-session behavior rather than relying on W + R arithmetic alone.”

</InterviewAnswer>
