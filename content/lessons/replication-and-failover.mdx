<BigPicture>

Replication creates additional copies for availability, read scale, and disaster recovery, but it does not make every copy equally current or authoritative. The design must name who may accept writes, which acknowledgements make a commit durable, how stale reads may be, and how an old primary is prevented from returning after failover.

For TokenX, a balance check immediately after a transfer needs read-your-writes behavior and usually routes to the leader or waits for a replica to reach the commit log position. An Ad Click Aggregator dashboard can often tolerate seconds of lag. Applying one routing rule to both either violates correctness or wastes capacity.

</BigPicture>

<UnderTheHood>

### From commit to replica

In leader-follower replication, the leader validates a transaction, records changes in a write-ahead log (WAL), makes the commit durable according to its policy, and streams ordered log records to followers. Each follower persists and replays the log. Track at least the leader commit position, follower received position, durable position, and applied position; “connected” does not mean “caught up.”

With asynchronous replication, the leader may acknowledge before any follower persists the record. Failover can therefore lose acknowledged writes. Synchronous acknowledgement lowers the loss window but adds network and follower storage latency to the commit path. A quorum configuration is safe only if membership, failure domains, and acknowledgement semantics are explicit.

### Lag and catch-up calculation

Assume the leader generates 15 MB/s of WAL and a follower is disconnected for 120 seconds. It accumulates `15 × 120 = 1.8 GB` of backlog. After reconnection it can ingest 45 MB/s while new WAL continues at 15 MB/s, leaving 30 MB/s of catch-up capacity. Ideal catch-up time is `1,800 / 30 = 60 seconds`, before replay CPU or I/O contention. During those 60 seconds, routing correctness-sensitive reads there is unsafe.

Lag is not just wall-clock time. Compare log positions and retained log availability. If required WAL has already been recycled, the replica must be re-seeded from a snapshot; retrying the stream cannot fill the gap.

### Read guarantees

- **Eventual reads:** route freely and tolerate bounded or measured staleness.
- **Read-your-writes:** attach the commit position to the session, then read from a replica only after it has applied that position, with a leader fallback.
- **Monotonic reads:** pin the session or carry a minimum position so it never observes an older snapshot.
- **Linearizable reads:** read through the current leader or use a quorum protocol that proves leadership and ordering.

Replication also multiplies bad state. An accidental delete, corrupt application write, or compromised credential is faithfully copied; backups and point-in-time recovery solve a different problem.

<RealExamples>

**TokenX transfer.** The POST response includes a transfer ID and commit position. A subsequent balance GET carries that position. A local replica may serve it only after applying the position; otherwise the router waits briefly or uses the leader. An idempotency key resolves a timeout where the client cannot tell whether commit occurred.

**Ad Click Aggregator.** Campaign dashboards read followers with a 10-second freshness SLO. If lag exceeds the threshold, show “data delayed” or route to another replica rather than silently presenting old totals as current. Raw events remain in the durable log so projections can be rebuilt.

**Regional failure.** Asynchronous cross-region replication may have an RPO greater than zero. If product claims zero loss, the design needs synchronous cross-region consensus and must accept the latency and availability consequences. DNS cutover alone is not fencing.

<InterviewTraps>

1. **“Replicas guarantee no data loss.”** Ask when success is returned and how many failure-independent copies have persisted the commit.
2. **“Promote when health checks fail.”** A slow node and a partition look alike. Promotion requires quorum and fencing, not a single observer's timeout.
3. **“Replica lag is two seconds.”** Specify received, durable, and applied positions; clock-based lag can hide a stalled stream with no new traffic.
4. **“Read replicas are transparent.”** State which endpoints tolerate stale data and how read-your-writes or monotonic sessions are enforced.
5. **“Replication is our backup.”** Replication propagates logical corruption and deletion. Tested, isolated recovery points are still necessary.

A strong IC6 answer includes the ambiguity window: after a leader dies, clients retry with stable idempotency keys while operators reconcile commits near the last durable position.

</InterviewTraps>

<Tradeoffs>

- Asynchronous replication preserves low write latency and regional independence but permits a nonzero RPO.
- Synchronous replication reduces acknowledged-write loss but makes commit latency and availability depend on the acknowledgement set.
- Automated failover shortens RTO but increases split-brain risk unless membership and fencing are rigorous.
- Serving reads from replicas increases capacity but requires explicit staleness, session, and overload policies.
- Multi-leader replication improves local write availability only for conflict-tolerant domains; balances and uniqueness invariants make merge semantics dangerous.

</Tradeoffs>

<InterviewAnswer>

“I define replication with positions and authority. The leader acknowledges according to a quantified durability policy, followers publish durable and applied positions, and the router enforces eventual, read-your-writes, or linearizable behavior per operation. I calculate backlog and catch-up headroom rather than calling a replica healthy because it is connected.

Failover requires quorum, fencing, a new epoch, idempotent retries, and reconciliation of ambiguous commits. Cross-region asynchronous copies imply nonzero RPO; synchronous copies add WAN latency and can reduce availability. Replication is not backup. See [indexes and query execution](/learn/indexes-and-query-execution), [transactions across boundaries](/learn/transactions-across-boundaries), and [backup and recovery](/learn/backup-restore-and-disaster-recovery).”
