<BigPicture>

High availability keeps serving through expected component failures. Disaster recovery reconstructs trustworthy service after region loss, operator error, corruption, compromise, or a failure that replication propagated. A backup is useful only if the organization can find it, decrypt it, restore it, validate it, and safely make it authoritative within the promised time.

Recovery Point Objective (RPO) is the maximum acceptable lost history; Recovery Time Objective (RTO) is the maximum acceptable time to restore service. TokenX may require near-zero loss for the ledger but tolerate rebuilding search for hours. The Ad Click Aggregator may accept five minutes of dashboard loss if durable raw clicks can replay. One global RPO/RTO hides these different business consequences.

</BigPicture>

<UnderTheHood>

### Recovery layers

A common database strategy combines periodic full or incremental snapshots with continuous archived WAL. Restore loads the latest usable snapshot before the target time, then replays WAL to a chosen point just before corruption or deletion. The snapshot and all required log segments must share compatible engine, schema, encryption, and checksum metadata.

Copies should cross failure boundaries: another account or project, another region, immutable retention, and tightly separated credentials. Offline or logically isolated copies reduce ransomware and operator-blast-radius risk. Replicas remain useful for availability but are not a substitute because they replicate bad writes.

### Throughput makes RTO concrete

A 20 TB database restored at a sustained 500 MB/s has an ideal transfer floor of about `20,000,000 MB / 500 MB/s = 40,000 seconds`, or 11.1 hours, before provisioning, decompression, WAL replay, index recovery, validation, and cutover. A four-hour RTO is impossible with that plan. Options include smaller independently restorable shards, warm standby, parallel restore, pre-staged snapshots, or a renegotiated objective.

If WAL is generated at 15 MB/s, one day is about `15 × 86,400 = 1.296 TB`. With a five-minute RPO, archive monitoring must detect and resolve a broken upload before more than `15 × 300 = 4.5 GB` of unprotected log accumulates. “Nightly backup” cannot meet that RPO.

### Dependencies and order

Restore identity and encryption dependencies before encrypted data, but protect keys separately so one compromised account cannot destroy both. Restore authoritative databases before derived search, caches, and analytics. Consumers need known replay positions; otherwise they may repeat external effects or skip events. Re-enable scheduled jobs, webhooks, email, and payment calls only after idempotency and cutover epochs are verified.

Backups need automated integrity checks plus regular full exercises. A checksum proves bytes are intact, not that schemas migrate, keys work, the application starts, or balances reconcile.

<RealExamples>

**TokenX point-in-time restore.** An operator runs an erroneous bulk update at 14:03. Restore the latest prior snapshot, replay WAL to just before the transaction, verify that debits equal credits per asset and idempotency records align, then reconcile legitimate transfers accepted after the recovery point from signed request logs or a surviving durable region. Do not silently discard them.

**Ad Click Aggregator rebuild.** If aggregate state is lost, restore raw immutable segments and Kafka positions, then recompute versioned minute totals. Dashboard service can return a delayed status while recovery proceeds. Billing remains closed until replay reaches the declared watermark and reconciliation totals match manifests.

**Ransomware scenario.** Same-account snapshots accessible by the production administrator may be deleted with production. Cross-account write-only backup roles, immutability locks, multi-party break-glass restore, and tested key escrow reduce correlated loss.

<InterviewTraps>

1. **“We take daily backups.”** Ask for RPO, archive frequency, retention, isolation, and the last measured restore time.
2. **“Replication gives zero RPO.”** Confirm acknowledgement semantics and failure domains; replication also copies deletion and corruption.
3. **“RTO starts after infrastructure is ready.”** Business RTO includes detection, decision, provisioning, restore, replay, validation, DNS or routing, and reconciliation.
4. **“Checksums validate recovery.”** They validate bytes, not application invariants, schema compatibility, credentials, or external effects.
5. **“Switch DNS to the backup.”** First fence old writers and establish a higher epoch; DNS caches do not prevent split brain.
6. **“Restore everything together.”** Prioritize authoritative tiers and degrade derived features so the critical path is smaller.

An IC6 response produces an evidence-based runbook and names the human decision points, not merely a storage feature list.

</InterviewTraps>

<Tradeoffs>

- Frequent snapshots reduce log replay but increase snapshot I/O and storage; continuous logs are still needed for fine-grained point-in-time recovery.
- Warm standby shortens RTO but costs more and can replicate corruption; isolated backups remain necessary.
- Immutable retention protects against deletion but can conflict with privacy erasure unless retention and key-destruction policies are designed together.
- Smaller shards restore in parallel but increase coordination and cross-shard consistency work.
- Automated cutover is fast for well-understood failures; corruption incidents often require a deliberate recovery point and human validation.

</Tradeoffs>

<InterviewAnswer>

“I classify data by business impact and assign measured RPO and RTO per tier. For authoritative state I combine compatible snapshots with continuous WAL in a cross-account immutable vault, separated key recovery, archive-gap alerts, and retention that includes privacy and legal obligations.

I calculate the transfer and replay lower bounds; a 20 TB restore at 500 MB/s already exceeds 11 hours before validation, so a four-hour RTO requires warm or parallel recovery architecture. Restore happens without external side effects, then checksums, application tests, ledger invariants, fencing, and reconciliation gate cutover. Related lessons are [replication](/learn/replication-and-failover), [object storage](/learn/object-storage-and-large-files), and [transactions](/learn/transactions-across-boundaries).”
