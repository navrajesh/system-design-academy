<BigPicture title="A distributed invariant needs a protocol, not a retry loop">

Once one business operation changes independently committed resources, a local rollback cannot undo the whole operation. Two-phase commit (2PC) coordinates an atomic decision among transactional participants. A saga commits a sequence of local steps and uses compensating business actions. Escrow and reservations divide a scarce invariant so participants can act locally within allocated rights.

TokenX should keep a same-shard transfer inside one ledger transaction. Only when ownership truly crosses shards or external providers should the design pay for a distributed protocol. The goal is not “microservice purity”; it is preserving conservation, uniqueness, and user-visible outcome under every crash point.

</BigPicture>

<UnderTheHood title="Atomic commit and durable workflows">

### Two-phase commit

In phase one, a coordinator asks each participant to prepare. A prepared participant durably records its tentative changes and promise not to abort, often retaining locks. If every participant votes yes, the coordinator durably records COMMIT and sends the decision; otherwise it records ABORT. In phase two, participants apply that decision and acknowledge.

2PC can provide atomicity but may block and retain locks when the decision is unavailable. Replicating the coordinator improves availability but does not remove participant coupling, latency, or operational recovery requirements.

### Sagas, reservations, and escrow

A saga persists a state machine before issuing each idempotent step. If a later step fails, it executes compensations in a defined order. Compensation is not rollback: shipping cannot be unshipped, exchange rates change, and refunds can fail. Put irreversible actions late and include terminal states such as `MANUAL_REVIEW`.

For a cross-shard TokenX transfer of 100 units, the invariant is conservation plus uniqueness: those 100 units are reserved on A, credited on B, or returned to A—never both spent on A and credited on B, and never credited twice. Each shard's ledger is authoritative for its own rows. The coordinator is not. Durability means a state is committed in that shard's log or database; an RPC response is not a source of truth.

Use one stable `transfer_id` on every message. Shard B stores one row keyed by `transfer_id` with durable states `ABSENT → CREDITED | CANCELLED`. `CREDITED` and `CANCELLED` are mutually exclusive terminals. Credit and cancel compete in **one serializable destination transaction**: compare-and-set from `ABSENT`, or insert under a unique constraint, then return the committed row. Duplicates return that same outcome. A delayed credit after `CANCELLED` is rejected. A delayed cancel after `CREDITED` cannot reverse the credit. Cancel of an `ABSENT` id still writes a durable `CANCELLED` tombstone so a later credit cannot sneak in.

Do not use a provisional, spendable destination credit unless you specify a second exclusive protocol (held funds, reverse journal, and a total order with finalize). This lesson uses the simpler terminal-credit model: B either commits a final credit or a cancel tombstone.

Shard A creates the reservation with an atomic insert unique on `transfer_id` (a unique constraint or insert-if-absent). Concurrent reserve attempts for the same id must not create two `RESERVED` rows; losers see the existing reservation. After that insert, the row is `RESERVED → FINALIZED | RELEASED`. Those terminals are mutually exclusive: there is no path that can reach both. Each change is an atomic conditional update (`UPDATE ... WHERE transfer_id = ? AND state = 'RESERVED'`). Finalize consumes the reservation into a debit. Release returns the 100 units to available balance.

A deadline does not prove that B failed. If confirmation is missing, the coordinator persists `UNRESOLVED` and queries B for the committed `transfer_id` row. If B is `CREDITED`, finalize A. If B is `CANCELLED`, release A. If B is still `ABSENT` or the query times out, remain `UNRESOLVED` and retry. Never treat an RPC timeout, a cache miss, or “no credit seen yet” as cancellation. Coordinator epochs still fence stale workers, but they do not replace B's tombstone: a late credit after A has `RELEASED` mints money.

Escrow allocates bounded rights: if a global inventory has 10,000 units, region West may receive 4,000 sale rights and East 6,000. Each can sell locally without coordination until its allocation is exhausted. Rebalancing rights needs coordination, and the sum of outstanding rights plus sold units must preserve the invariant.

At 2,000 workflows/s and a 20-minute downstream outage, the durable workflow table accumulates 2.4 million active or retryable items. At 1 KB each that is roughly 2.4 GB before indexes. Recovery capacity must exceed new arrivals; an 8,000/s worker drains backlog at 6,000/s net in about 400 seconds.

</UnderTheHood>

<MentalModel title="Atomic vote versus checked itinerary">

2PC is a group signing one contract: everyone prepares, then one recorded decision binds all. A saga is a travel itinerary: each booking is real, and cancellation follows explicit policies rather than erasing history. Escrow is distributing a fixed set of numbered tickets so local sellers cannot oversell.

Retain: keep invariants local first; otherwise choose atomic blocking, visible intermediate workflow, or preallocated rights consciously.

</MentalModel>

>

Shard A owns one reservation row per `transfer_id`, inserted uniquely, then `RESERVED → FINALIZED | RELEASED`. Shard B owns `ABSENT → CREDITED | CANCELLED`. Each pair is a fork, not a chain: the two terminals cannot both be reached. Each change is one local serializable transaction on that shard. The coordinator owns only workflow state, including `UNRESOLVED`; it cannot write balances. Timeout is unknown: query B's committed `transfer_id` row. `CREDITED` authorizes finalize; `CANCELLED` (a tombstone, even if no credit ever existed) authorizes release. `ABSENT` or a lost query keeps `UNRESOLVED`. Dashed edges are rejected commands, not state transitions: a late credit is rejected against a tombstone, and a late cancel is rejected against a committed credit. Neither dashed edge moves B from `CANCELLED` to `CREDITED` or the reverse. Reconciliation compares both ledgers and escalates unexplained divergence.

<RealExamples title="Atomicity where needed, workflows where visible">

**TokenX.** Prefer co-location and one transaction for common transfers. For cross-shard flow, A inserts one unique `RESERVED` row, B commits `CREDITED` or `CANCELLED`, and A then `FINALIZED` or `RELEASED`. Expose `PENDING` and `UNRESOLVED` honestly. If product requires instantaneous all-or-nothing balances across shards, use a database that supports that distributed transaction or redesign ownership rather than disguising a saga.

**Ad Click billing.** Closing a campaign period, producing an invoice, and charging a provider is a saga. The closed aggregate version is immutable; invoice creation uses a unique period key; provider calls carry an idempotency key. A correction produces an adjustment, not deletion of an already issued invoice.

<InterviewTraps title="Distributed transaction shortcuts that fail">

1. **“2PC rolls back after prepare.”** A YES voter promised not to abort independently; unilateral rollback can violate atomicity.
2. **“Sagas are eventually consistent transactions.”** They expose real intermediate states and require business compensation, not database rollback.
3. **“Retry the remote call.”** Timeout is an unknown outcome; retry only with stable identity and queryable durable result.
4. **“Release the reservation when the deadline fires.”** Deadline expiry is still unknown. Release only after B's durable `CANCELLED` tombstone; absence of a credit row is not cancellation.
5. **“No credit means cancel.”** Cancel must persist `CANCELLED` even when B was `ABSENT`. Otherwise a delayed credit still commits.
6. **“Compensation always succeeds.”** Model retry exhaustion, irreversible actions, changed prices, and manual resolution.
7. **“Reserve with a cache TTL.”** The authoritative ledger must own reservation amount and the exclusive `FINALIZED`/`RELEASED` transition atomically.
8. **“Consensus solves cross-shard atomicity.”** Each shard may have consensus internally; atomic agreement across groups is still another protocol.
9. **“Dual write then repair.”** That is acceptable only if the invariant tolerates the inconsistent interval and repair semantics are explicit.

</InterviewTraps>

<Tradeoffs title="Coordination versus visible intermediate state">

- 2PC provides atomic commit across compatible participants but adds synchronous latency, lock duration, and blocking recovery.
- Sagas scale organizationally and allow independent commits, but users observe pending states and compensation can be incomplete.
- Reservations bound overspend while permitting asynchronous completion, at the cost of temporarily unavailable capacity and an explicit `UNRESOLVED` wait until B's terminal state is known.
- Escrow enables local availability for divisible invariants, but rights allocation can become skewed and rebalancing needs coordination.
- Co-locating an invariant often beats every distributed protocol, but may create hot shards or constrain placement.

</Tradeoffs>

<InterviewAnswer>

“I first try to keep the invariant in one transaction. When participants must commit independently, 2PC gives atomic agreement but can block after prepare and holds resources across failures. A saga instead persists a workflow of idempotent local commits and domain compensations, exposing pending states honestly.

For cross-shard TokenX I keep conservation with exclusive terminals: B is `ABSENT → CREDITED | CANCELLED` under one serializable decision on `transfer_id`; A inserts one `RESERVED` row uniquely on `transfer_id`, then `RESERVED → FINALIZED | RELEASED` by conditional update. Timeout is `UNRESOLVED`: I query B's committed row, finalize if `CREDITED`, release only if `CANCELLED`, and retry if unknown. A tombstone is required even when no credit existed, because absence is not a fence. Escrow is useful when a divisible global limit can be preallocated. See [transactions across boundaries](/learn/transactions-across-boundaries), [consensus](/learn/consensus-and-leader-election), and [idempotency](/learn/idempotency-and-deduplication).”

</InterviewAnswer>
