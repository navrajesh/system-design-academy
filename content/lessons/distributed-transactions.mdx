<BigPicture title="A distributed invariant needs a protocol, not a retry loop">

Once one business operation changes independently committed resources, a local rollback cannot undo the whole operation. Two-phase commit (2PC) coordinates an atomic decision among transactional participants. A saga commits a sequence of local steps and uses compensating business actions. Escrow and reservations divide a scarce invariant so participants can act locally within allocated rights.

TokenX should keep a same-shard transfer inside one ledger transaction. Only when ownership truly crosses shards or external providers should the design pay for a distributed protocol. The goal is not “microservice purity”; it is preserving conservation, uniqueness, and user-visible outcome under every crash point.

</BigPicture>

<UnderTheHood title="Atomic commit and durable workflows">

### Two-phase commit

In phase one, a coordinator asks each participant to prepare. A prepared participant durably records its tentative changes and promise not to abort, often retaining locks. If every participant votes yes, the coordinator durably records COMMIT and sends the decision; otherwise it records ABORT. In phase two, participants apply that decision and acknowledge.

2PC can provide atomicity but may block and retain locks when the decision is unavailable. Replicating the coordinator improves availability but does not remove participant coupling, latency, or operational recovery requirements.

### Sagas, reservations, and escrow

A saga persists a state machine before issuing each idempotent step. If a later step fails, it executes compensations in a defined order. Compensation is not rollback: shipping cannot be unshipped, exchange rates change, and refunds can fail. Put irreversible actions late and include terminal states such as `MANUAL_REVIEW`.

For a cross-shard TokenX transfer of 100 units, shard A can append a 100-unit reservation that reduces available balance without finalizing the debit. Shard B accepts one credit command keyed by transfer ID. After the credit is durably confirmed, A converts the reservation to a final debit. If the deadline expires before credit, A releases it. A fencing epoch prevents an old coordinator from finalizing after release.

Escrow allocates bounded rights: if a global inventory has 10,000 units, region West may receive 4,000 sale rights and East 6,000. Each can sell locally without coordination until its allocation is exhausted. Rebalancing rights needs coordination, and the sum of outstanding rights plus sold units must preserve the invariant.

At 2,000 workflows/s and a 20-minute downstream outage, the durable workflow table accumulates 2.4 million active or retryable items. At 1 KB each that is roughly 2.4 GB before indexes. Recovery capacity must exceed new arrivals; an 8,000/s worker drains backlog at 6,000/s net in about 400 seconds.

</UnderTheHood>

<MentalModel title="Atomic vote versus checked itinerary">

2PC is a group signing one contract: everyone prepares, then one recorded decision binds all. A saga is a travel itinerary: each booking is real, and cancellation follows explicit policies rather than erasing history. Escrow is distributing a fixed set of numbered tickets so local sellers cannot oversell.

Retain: keep invariants local first; otherwise choose atomic blocking, visible intermediate workflow, or preallocated rights consciously.

</MentalModel>

Shard A owns the spendable-balance reservation; shard B owns the credited entry after its local commit. The saga store owns workflow state, deadlines, and epochs, but it cannot overwrite either ledger. Stable transfer IDs make messages replay-safe. Reconciliation compares saga state with both immutable ledgers and escalates unexplained divergence.

<RealExamples title="Atomicity where needed, workflows where visible">

**TokenX.** Prefer co-location and one transaction for common transfers. For cross-shard flow, reservation plus idempotent credit exposes `PENDING` honestly. If product requires an instantaneous all-or-nothing balance across shards, use a database that supports the required distributed transaction or redesign ownership rather than disguising a saga.

**Ad Click billing.** Closing a campaign period, producing an invoice, and charging a provider is a saga. The closed aggregate version is immutable; invoice creation uses a unique period key; provider calls carry an idempotency key. A correction produces an adjustment, not deletion of an already issued invoice.

<InterviewTraps title="Distributed transaction shortcuts that fail">

1. **“2PC rolls back after prepare.”** A YES voter promised not to abort independently; unilateral rollback can violate atomicity.
2. **“Sagas are eventually consistent transactions.”** They expose real intermediate states and require business compensation, not database rollback.
3. **“Retry the remote call.”** Timeout is an unknown outcome; retry only with stable identity and queryable durable result.
4. **“Compensation always succeeds.”** Model retry exhaustion, irreversible actions, changed prices, and manual resolution.
5. **“Reserve with a cache TTL.”** The authoritative ledger must own reservation amount, version, and release transition atomically.
6. **“Consensus solves cross-shard atomicity.”** Each shard may have consensus internally; atomic agreement across groups is still another protocol.
7. **“Dual write then repair.”** That is acceptable only if the invariant tolerates the inconsistent interval and repair semantics are explicit.

</InterviewTraps>

<Tradeoffs title="Coordination versus visible intermediate state">

- 2PC provides atomic commit across compatible participants but adds synchronous latency, lock duration, and blocking recovery.
- Sagas scale organizationally and allow independent commits, but users observe pending states and compensation can be incomplete.
- Reservations bound overspend while permitting asynchronous completion, at the cost of temporarily unavailable capacity and expiry races.
- Escrow enables local availability for divisible invariants, but rights allocation can become skewed and rebalancing needs coordination.
- Co-locating an invariant often beats every distributed protocol, but may create hot shards or constrain placement.

</Tradeoffs>

<InterviewAnswer>

“I first try to keep the invariant in one transaction. When participants must commit independently, 2PC gives atomic agreement but can block after prepare and holds resources across failures. A saga instead persists a workflow of idempotent local commits and domain compensations, exposing pending states honestly.

For cross-shard TokenX I use a source-ledger reservation, destination credit keyed by transfer ID, fenced finalize or release, and reconciliation. Timeout is an unknown outcome, so I query or retry the same identity rather than assume failure. Escrow is useful when a divisible global limit can be preallocated. See [transactions across boundaries](/learn/transactions-across-boundaries), [consensus](/learn/consensus-and-leader-election), and [idempotency](/learn/idempotency-and-deduplication).”

</InterviewAnswer>
