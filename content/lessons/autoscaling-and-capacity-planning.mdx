<BigPicture title="Scale the queue that is actually the bottleneck">

Autoscaling adds **replicas of a homogeneous, mostly stateless pool** after a signal says the current pool is short. Capacity planning decides the **floor, ceiling, and headroom** those replicas are allowed to occupy, including the pieces that **do not** autoscale: a single-primary database, a shard map, a Kafka partition count, a broker disk, an account row lock.

[Scaling read-heavy services](/learn/scaling-read-heavy-services) already showed that ten API boxes do nothing if the origin is saturated. This lesson adds the control loop: which metric, how much spare, how long until a replica is useful, and what a scale-out **amplifies** downstream.

Name the two math envelopes up front, with units:

- **Rate envelope:** peak ≈ average × seasonal or burst factor (requests/s, records/s, bytes/s).
- **Concurrency envelope:** in-flight L = λW (Little’s Law) for a named boundary—API, connection pool, or consumer.

TokenX: autoscale the **stateless transfer API** before you pressure the **uniqueness store** and **ledger primary**. Never autoscale into a hot ledger row; that row is one owner, as in [Write scaling](/learn/write-scaling). Ad Click: autoscale **collectors** from ingest queue depth, and **consumers** from lag—separately. Consumer replicas above the **partition count** stay idle ([Ordering and event keys](/learn/ordering-and-event-keys) and consumer-group ownership).

</BigPicture>

<UnderTheHood title="Signals, warm-up, packing, and the math with units">

### Leading versus lagging signals

A **leading** signal moves **before** user-visible SLO burn: inbound queue depth, Kafka consumer lag, request wait time in the load balancer, TokenX checkout latency excluding a known slow dependency, billed-click produce buffer occupancy.

A **lagging** signal moves **after** the pool is already late: average CPU, memory, RPS **completed** (not offered), disk fullness, error rate. CPU is useful as a **cap** and a cost signal. It is a poor sole trigger for a JVM service whose CPU is 40% while the ledger pool is 95% busy, or for a consumer whose CPU is idle while lag is 12 minutes.

RPS as a scale metric is ambiguous: completed RPS can **fall** on overload while offered RPS rises. Prefer **queue depth + latency + lag**, with CPU as a sanity bound. Checkout latency is leading only if you measure the **queueing** component, not just the ledger’s 15 ms row hold.

### Headroom, warm-up, cooldown, scale-to-zero

**Headroom** is spare capacity you pay for so the next burst fits **before** new replicas become ready. If warm-up is 90 s and lag grows at 8,000 records/s, 90 × 8,000 = 720,000 extra records land first.

**Warm-up** is not “the container started.” JVM JIT, HTTP/2 and database **connection pools**, TLS sessions, and **cache fill** all happen after `Ready`. A replica that is Ready with an empty cache can **stampede** the primary—the scale-out **amplifies** database QPS, the opposite of [cache-aside](/learn/scaling-read-heavy-services).

**Cooldown** stops flapping: after a scale-out, wait before another; after a scale-in, wait longer. Aggressive scale-in during a brief trough dumps connection pools and cold caches right before the next spike.

**Scale-to-zero** traps: the first request pays cold start plus pool plus cache; token-bucket bursts collide; Kafka consumers that scale to zero **stop heartbeat/poll** and trigger a rebalance storm when they return; TokenX uniqueness checks cannot wait 8 s for a function cold start on a debit path. Keep a **min replica floor**.

**Min replicas for AZ failure:** if you need N serving replicas after losing one availability zone, floor is at least `ceil(N / (z-1))` per remaining math, deployed across z zones, not `N` in one zone with HPA hope. Illustrative: need 6 APIs after one of three AZs dies → at least 3 per AZ (9 total floor), not 6 total.

### HPA versus provisioned; bin packing; seasonal capacity

**Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA)** (or equivalent) can add/remove **stateless API** pods, **collector** pods, **consumer** pods, and—carefully—**cache cluster** nodes with a rebalance story. It cannot add **Postgres primary** writers, **shard count**, **Kafka partitions**, or **broker disks**. Those you **provision** on a lead time of minutes to weeks.

**Bin packing** is placing replicas onto nodes without stranding CPU, memory, or network. A 4 GiB consumer on a 5 GiB node wastes 1 GiB and may still OOM on a hot partition. Pack by the **scarce resource** (often memory for JVM consumers, disk for brokers) and keep disruption budgets so a node drain does not drop below the AZ floor.

**Seasonal capacity:** ads around a known event (illustrative 10× peak factor) should be **pre-provisioned** hours ahead. HPA reacting at kickoff is already late by the warm-up plus partition-skew term. TokenX month-end settlement is the same pattern: raise the API floor and connection-pool caps the day before, not when checkout latency trips.

<Calculation label="Peak rate, in-flight, and a 90-second warm-up">

Assumptions (illustrative): Ad Click average 20,000 billed clicks/s over a day is the wrong planning base if you only have a daily total.

Better: 864 million clicks/day ÷ 86,400 s/day ≈ 10,000 clicks/s **average**. Peak factor 8 → peak ≈ 80,000 clicks/s.

Payload 400 B → peak leader ingress 80,000 × 400 B = 32 MB/s; ×3 replication ≈ 96 MB/s broker write (before protocol overhead).

Little’s Law on collectors: λ = 80,000 /s, W = 25 ms in-collector (batch linger + network) → L = 80,000 × 0.025 = 2,000 clicks in flight in collectors.

Consumer lag: apply 60,000 /s at peak 80,000 /s → surplus 20,000 /s. After 90 s warm-up, lag ≈ 1.8 million records **before** new consumers take partitions. If the topic has 40 partitions, adding a 41st consumer does not split the hot campaign partition.

TokenX API: 2,000 transfers/s × 0.20 s end-to-end → 400 in flight. Uniqueness store hold 8 ms on 2,000 /s → 16 busy connections on average. Headroom to 40 connections is ~2.5× on the mean, less on p95. Scaling APIs from 6 to 18 does **not** multiply that 16; it can multiply **offered** attempts into the same primary.

</Calculation>

### Cache stampede and database amplification

Scale-out of **API** replicas with empty caches turns a 90% hit rate into a miss storm. Origin QPS jumps toward the full read rate (the cache-load envelope in the read-scaling lesson). Mitigations: warm caches before marking Ready, request coalescing, TTL jitter, and **rate-limit new replicas’ origin traffic**. Scale **cache cluster** separately from APIs so you do not share one HPA.

TokenX: new API pods that retry uniqueness inserts under a timeout multiply load into the uniqueness store. Shed with admission and [Graceful degradation](/learn/graceful-degradation); do not HPA the API on CPU while the primary’s lock wait is the real signal.

Ads: scale **collectors** on ingest queue depth (leading). Scale **consumers** on **lag** and sink SLO, capped at partition count. Provision partitions (and brokers) when lag stays high with one owner per partition.

<MentalModel title="Hire cashiers that can actually take a drawer">

HPA is a recruiter who can wheel in extra cashiers (stateless APIs, collectors, consumers). It cannot 3D-print a second vault door (single primary), split one crowded drawer into two (a hot ledger row / one Kafka partition), or fill every new cashier’s till with cached bills (warm-up). Seasonal Saturday is a **scheduled hire**, not a surprise at 10:01.

Queue depth is the line you can see forming. CPU is how sweaty someone looks after they have already been serving. Little’s Law counts bodies **in the store** (λW); peak factor stretches **arrivals**. Scale-to-zero is sending everyone home and hoping the first customer enjoys unlocking the building.

Retain: **scale homogeneous pools from leading queues, provision authorities and partitions, keep AZ floors and warm-up headroom, and never autoscale a lock.**

</MentalModel>

**Authoritative state:** TokenX uniqueness store plus ledger primary (one writer), and the Ad Click billed-click log until retention. **Derived state:** API caches, dashboards, and consumer apply progress (lag). **Consistency boundary:** autoscaling never moves money or invoice identity; those commit on the provisioned authorities. HPA may add API, collector, consumer, and cache-cluster replicas. Shards, brokers, partition count, and the ledger primary are **provisioned**. Solid edges are serving and scale-out of pools. Dashed edges are cooldown holds (reject extra churn), cache-miss stampede into the primary (failure amplification), and extra consumers idle on an unchanged partition count. Recovery is warm before Ready, admit/shed into the uniqueness store, pre-provision seasonal partitions, and fail closed on TokenX if the primary cannot take more—not “add pods until CPU falls.”

<RealExamples title="TokenX scales APIs first; ads scale collectors and consumers on different signals">

**TokenX.** Scale the **stateless API** on checkout **wait time** and offered RPS, with a min floor for AZ loss. Watch **uniqueness store** connections and ledger lock wait as **saturation of an authority you provision** (bigger primary, read-only offload of non-spendable reads, or shard by account—not HPA). Do not autoscale into a hot treasury row: more pods retrying the same `UPDATE` make Little’s Law worse. Uniqueness and money stay fail-closed if the primary is full ([Graceful degradation](/learn/graceful-degradation)). Replication and failover of that primary are [Replication and failover](/learn/replication-and-failover), not an HPA tick. Cache only metadata; a cold-cache scale-out must not become a spendable-balance origin storm.

**Ad Click Aggregator.** **Collectors** autoscale on **ingest queue depth** (leading) and are allowed to grow with offered click rate. They still ACK after the billed-fact log, so collector scale cannot outrun **broker** provision. **Consumers** autoscale on **lag** up to **partition count**; beyond that, provision partitions (and accept a rebalance). A viral campaign remains one partition: extra members idle. Do not couple collector HPA to consumer HPA—the collector can be CPU-busy on compression while consumers wait on a sink. Seasonal product launches: raise partition count and broker disk **the day before**, then let HPA fill consumers to that new cap.

<InterviewTraps title="Capacity slogans that fail at IC6">

- **“We autoscale the database.”** You autoscale **clients** of a single primary until it dies. Secondaries are reads. Shards are a provisioned topology.
- **“CPU at 70% is the SLO.”** CPU can be low while queues and lock wait burn the latency SLO.
- **“Peak equals average.”** Daily totals hide an 8× hour. Always state the factor and the window.
- **“In-flight is RPS times replica count.”** In-flight is λW on a **named** boundary. Replica count is not W.
- **“More consumers always drain lag.”** After partition count, they idle or thrash rebalances.
- **“Ready means warm.”** Empty caches and closed pools make scale-out a stampede.
- **“Scale-to-zero is free.”** The first debit or billed batch pays cold start; Kafka membership flaps.
- **“One HPA for the whole chart.”** APIs, caches, collectors, and consumers have different leading signals and different authorities behind them.
- **“Autoscaling gives us linearizability of capacity.”** There is no such property. There is a control loop with delay, hysteresis, and downstream amplification.

</InterviewTraps>

<Tradeoffs title="Paying for headroom versus paying for delay">

- **Leading versus lagging metrics:** queues scale earlier and can overshoot on a brief spike; CPU is cheaper to scrape and late.
- **Headroom versus cost:** a warm min floor wastes idle cores and saves SLO during AZ loss and warm-up. Seasonal pre-provision wastes a weekend and beats 90 s of 8× lag.
- **HPA versus provisioned:** HPA is minutes and reversible; partitions, shards, and primary size are slow, operationally heavier, and actually raise write ownership.
- **Scale-out versus stampede:** faster HPA without warm-up hurts the authority you were trying to protect.
- **Cooldown versus agility:** long cooldown misses a second peak; short cooldown flaps pools and DNS.
- **Bin-pack density versus blast radius:** tighter packing raises utilization and makes one node drain an AZ-floor breach.

Do not trade TokenX uniqueness or billed-fact durability for a prettier CPU graph. Trade dashboard completeness and debug ingest first.

</Tradeoffs>

<InterviewAnswer>

“I plan two envelopes: peak rate as average times a named burst factor, with bytes per event, and in-flight L equals lambda W on a named boundary—not replica count. Autoscaling adds homogeneous pools: TokenX APIs, ad collectors, consumers, maybe cache nodes. It does not add a second Postgres primary, extra shards, or Kafka partitions. I scale on leading signals—queue depth, checkout wait, consumer lag—not completed RPS or CPU alone, because completed RPS can fall under overload. Headroom covers warm-up: JIT, connection pools, cache fill. Ready is not warm; a cold scale-out can stampede the ledger. Min replicas follow AZ-failure math, not scale-to-zero on debit or billed ingest. Ads: collectors and consumers have separate HPAs; consumers cap at partition count. TokenX: scale APIs before the uniqueness store; never autoscale into a hot ledger row. Seasonal peaks I provision ahead of the 90-second lag spike. Cooldown stops flapping. If the primary is the bottleneck I shed or fail closed rather than add pods.”

</InterviewAnswer>
