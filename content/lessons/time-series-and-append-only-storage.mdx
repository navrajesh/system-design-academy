<BigPicture>

Time-series systems optimize for a dominant shape: records arrive continually, recent time ranges are hot, old data becomes colder, and retention or aggregation changes its resolution. Append-only storage turns random mutation into sequential writes and an auditable history, but derived answers still need correction policies when events arrive late or duplicated.

The Ad Click Aggregator stores raw click facts before producing minute and campaign totals. TokenX uses an immutable ledger for financial history. Both append facts, but their truth models differ: ad totals can be recomputed from an event log, while a TokenX ledger entry is authoritative and corrections are new compensating entries, never edits to history.

</BigPicture>

<UnderTheHood>

### Three clocks and an ordered identity

- **Event time** says when the click or transfer happened at the source.
- **Ingestion time** says when the platform durably accepted it.
- **Processing time** says when a consumer computed a result.

Use a stable event ID for deduplication and retain both event and ingestion timestamps. Device clocks can be wrong, so event time alone cannot define operational retention or idempotency. Within a TokenX account, an assigned monotonic `account_sequence` provides deterministic ordering even when timestamps collide.

### Write path and immutable segments

Many time-series engines append first to a WAL, update an in-memory structure, flush sorted immutable segments, then compact segments in the background. Reads merge memory and overlapping segments. Bloom filters can avoid unnecessary segment reads for point keys; min/max time metadata prunes ranges. Tombstones represent deletion until compaction, so a delete is neither immediate space reclamation nor necessarily cheap.

### Capacity and retention

At 100 million clicks per day, average ingress is about `100,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ 1,157 events/s`. A 12× peak is about 13,900 events/s. If an encoded event averages 800 bytes, raw volume is 80 GB/day. Thirty days is 2.4 TB raw; replication factor three makes 7.2 TB before indexes and metadata. At 3:1 compression, the replicated payload is roughly 2.4 TB, but budget additional headroom for compaction that temporarily holds old and new segments.

<InteractiveCalculation mode="event-ingress" initialRate={100000000} initialEventBytes={800} initialPeakFactor={12} />

Rollups radically change the long-term footprint. One row per campaign per minute for 200,000 active campaigns is up to 288 million rows/day if every campaign is active every minute—often much larger than expected. Sparse activity and coarser hourly rollups must be measured, not assumed.

### Partitioning and late data

Partition by a bounded time unit for pruning and lifecycle operations, then distribute within it by a stable entity hash when write volume demands it. Partitioning only by timestamp funnels current writes to one shard; partitioning only by campaign can make retention deletes expensive. Watermarks are a product contract: a five-minute watermark means most answers stabilize after five minutes, not that later events disappear. Corrections need versioning and downstream upsert semantics.

<RealExamples>

**Ad Click Aggregator.** Store `event_id`, campaign, creative, event time, ingestion time, cost attributes, and schema version. Deduplicate for the advertised retry window. Dashboard rows carry `(campaign, minute, version)` so corrections replace an older result rather than double-add it.

**TokenX ledger.** Append balanced debit and credit entries in the authoritative transaction. A reversal is another transfer linked to the original. A searchable wallet-history projection may use the same append-and-replay techniques, but deleting or rebuilding that projection cannot alter balances.

**When it fails.** High-cardinality labels such as raw user IDs in every metric create enormous indexes and fan-out. Tiny time partitions overload metadata. Huge partitions make retention and repair slow. Compaction without I/O limits can starve foreground ingestion. A retention job that deletes the hot store but not archives or backups violates the data policy.

<InterviewTraps>

1. **“Timestamp is the event ID.”** Clocks collide and retry; carry an independently stable identifier.
2. **“Exactly-once means no deduplication.”** End-to-end side effects still require idempotent keys and transactional boundaries.
3. **“Close the window and ignore late events.”** State the allowed lateness, correction path, and product behavior for final reports.
4. **“Compression makes storage negligible.”** Include replicas, indexes, compaction headroom, metadata, and backups.
5. **“Append-only means nothing is deleted.”** Retention, privacy erasure, cryptographic deletion, tombstones, and backup expiry still need an enforceable policy.

At IC6, distinguish authoritative facts from disposable views and state how every derived view is rebuilt and corrected.

</InterviewTraps>

<Tradeoffs>

- Fine time buckets improve pruning and deletion but increase partitions, metadata, and small files.
- Longer deduplication windows handle delayed retries but require more state and may still not cover archival replay.
- Longer watermarks reduce corrections but increase result latency.
- LSM-style writes favor ingestion but cause read amplification and background compaction cost.
- Raw retention preserves audit and recomputation options; it becomes inappropriate when privacy, cost, or business policy demands earlier deletion.

</Tradeoffs>

<InterviewAnswer>

“I make the durable append log the accepted-event boundary, retain event and ingestion time plus a stable ID, and build versioned aggregates as replayable projections. For 100 million 800-byte clicks per day I budget 80 GB/day raw, then replicas, indexes, compression, compaction headroom, and backups separately.

Partitions combine time for lifecycle management with a distribution key for write spread. Watermarks define normal result latency; late events follow a correction stream with idempotent replacement semantics. The same append shape supports TokenX history, but ledger entries are authoritative and corrections are compensating entries. Related lessons are [partitioning](/learn/partitioning-vs-sharding-vs-aggregation), [object storage](/learn/object-storage-and-large-files), and [Kafka delivery](/learn/kafka-delivery-and-failure).”

</InterviewAnswer>
