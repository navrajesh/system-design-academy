<BigPicture title="Consensus chooses one history, not merely one server">

Consensus lets independent nodes agree on an ordered sequence despite crashes and delayed messages. Leader election is one part: a leader proposes log entries, but safety comes from terms, voting restrictions, quorum intersection, log matching, and commit rules. A health check selecting “the fastest node” is not consensus.

TokenX may use a consensus-backed database so committed ledger mutations survive leader failure with one authoritative order. The Ad Click Aggregator may use consensus for broker metadata and partition leadership even though individual aggregate values are eventually consistent.

</BigPicture>

<UnderTheHood title="Terms, votes, logs, and commit">

In a Raft-style protocol, each node persists its current term, vote, and log. A follower that misses heartbeats starts an election after a randomized timeout, increments its term, votes for itself, and requests votes. A voter grants at most one vote per term and only to a candidate whose log is at least as up to date. In a five-node cluster, three votes elect a leader and the system tolerates two crash failures while retaining progress.

Consensus safety does not require bounded network delay: two different values must not commit at the same log position. Liveness does require eventual timing conditions—messages must eventually arrive and one candidate must stay connected to a quorum long enough. During a long partition, the minority remains safe but unavailable for new commits.

### Commit is more than replica count

A leader normally commits a current-term entry after a majority stores it; earlier entries then become committed through log ordering. Simply finding an old entry on a majority at different times can be unsafe if terms and election rules are ignored.

Reads need separate authority. A committed no-op proves the leader held a majority at the moment that no-op committed; it is not a standing license to serve later local reads.

A new leader first establishes **current-term commit knowledge**. Prior-term entries become committed through that current-term commit (often a no-op). That step makes `commitIndex` safe for this term; it does not authorize later reads by itself.

Then a linearizable read proceeds as follows:

1. Verify **current leader authority**. For ReadIndex, send **current-term** heartbeats (or equivalent AppendEntries carrying this term) and wait for a quorum that still recognizes this node as leader **for this term**. For a lease, hold a still-valid leader lease under an explicit **bounded-clock** assumption and stop lease reads the instant renewal cannot be confirmed. If this node observes a **higher term** in a heartbeat response, RequestVote, or AppendEntries, it must **step down**, adopt that term, and **refuse linearizable reads**. It does not keep serving as leader after seeing a newer term.
2. Determine the required **read index** (the `commitIndex` recorded at ReadIndex time, or the lease-protected commit index being served).
3. **Wait until `lastApplied >= readIndex`** so the state machine includes every committed entry the read is allowed to observe. The lease path skips the extra heartbeat, not this apply wait. A one-time no-op is not a perpetual read gate.
4. Only then serve the read from applied state.

Lease safety is a **bounded-clock** claim, not a Raft theorem: the lease duration you grant must stay strictly larger than the maximum clock drift you are willing to assume between nodes, otherwise a deposed leader's local clock can still treat an expired lease as live. That drift bound is an operational planning assumption. It is not a measured guarantee of the protocol. If clocks can violate the bound, fall back to ReadIndex or refuse the read.

Committing a current-term no-op is the Raft technique that allows committing prior-term entries. Do not treat that commit as ongoing read authority.

At 10,000 commands/s with 500-byte encoded log entries, payload append is 5 MB/s per replica before protocol and storage overhead. A five-node cluster writes roughly 25 MB/s of replica payload. Consensus is not a substitute for partitioning: one group still has one ordered commit stream and one hot leader path.

### Membership changes

Replacing three nodes at once can accidentally create an old majority and a disjoint new majority. Safe protocols use joint consensus or an equivalent staged configuration whose quorums overlap across old and new membership. Learners should treat “autoscale the consensus nodes” as a protocol operation, not ordinary instance replacement.

Retain: election chooses an epoch-scoped proposer; quorum plus log rules chooses history; fencing prevents obsolete authority from causing side effects.

The committed log and deterministic state machine own authoritative order. Followers own durable log prefixes, not independent truth. Linearizable reads are a separate consistency boundary: current-term commit knowledge, then ReadIndex with **current-term** quorum heartbeats (followers must still name this node leader for this term) or a still-valid **bounded-clock** lease, then wait until `lastApplied >= readIndex` on **both** paths, and only then serve from applied state. A previously committed no-op is not that gate. Seeing a higher term forces step-down and refused reads. The external sink must validate a fencing epoch or consume an idempotent committed command; otherwise an isolated old leader can still damage resources outside the consensus group.

<RealExamples title="Where consensus helps and where it stops">

**TokenX ledger shard.** One consensus group orders transfers for accounts in that shard. A committed idempotency record resolves client retries. Cross-shard transfers still need a distributed transaction, reservation, or redesigned ownership protocol; two independent consensus groups do not magically share one atomic log.

**Ad Click Aggregator.** Kafka-like brokers use a metadata quorum and per-partition leaders to decide ownership. Aggregation workers should not put every click through one global consensus group; the ordered partition log already provides the necessary local sequence.

<InterviewTraps title="Consensus claims that omit the proof">

1. **“Three replicas tolerate two failures.”** A three-member majority is two, so only one crash can be tolerated while making progress.
2. **“The newest timestamp wins the election.”** Election safety uses terms and log freshness, not unsynchronized wall clocks.
3. **“Replicated means committed.”** An entry may exist on followers yet remain overwriteable until the protocol's commit condition holds.
4. **“A committed no-op lets the leader serve local reads indefinitely.”** The no-op establishes current-term commit knowledge only at that instant. Later linearizable reads need current authority (ReadIndex with **current-term** quorum heartbeats, or a still-valid bounded-clock lease) **and** `lastApplied >= readIndex` on both paths.
5. **“A higher-term vote can be ignored while the lease is live.”** Observing a higher term in a heartbeat response, RequestVote, or AppendEntries requires **step-down**. Refuse linearizable reads. A lease does not override a newer term, and lease duration must remain strictly larger than the maximum clock drift you assume—an operational bound, not a protocol-measured guarantee.
6. **“Consensus prevents split brain everywhere.”** It controls participating state. External stores need fencing tokens or idempotent committed commands.
7. **“Add nodes for throughput.”** More members increase fault-domain options but add quorum latency and do not increase one log's ordered write capacity.
8. **“Replace the cluster in parallel.”** Membership transition must preserve quorum intersection.

</InterviewTraps>

<Tradeoffs title="Safety, latency, availability, and scope">

- Larger quorums tolerate more failures but add replication work and more slow-member exposure.
- Odd-sized voter sets usually buy more fault tolerance per member; learners should calculate majority explicitly.
- Leader leases can make reads faster by skipping per-read **current-term** quorum heartbeats, but they still wait until `lastApplied >= readIndex`. They depend on a **bounded-clock** assumption: lease duration must stay strictly larger than the maximum drift you are willing to assume. That inequality is a planning constraint, not a Raft-measured guarantee. ReadIndex costs a round trip and uses fewer clock assumptions. A higher term still forces step-down on either path.
- Consensus gives strong ordering inside one group; globalizing unrelated keys creates latency and throughput bottlenecks.
- Automatic reconfiguration helps operations but increases protocol risk; staged, observable membership changes are safer.

</Tradeoffs>

<InterviewAnswer>

“Consensus selects one ordered history through persistent terms, restricted voting, log matching, and overlapping quorums. In a five-node group, three durable acknowledgements form a majority and two crashes can be tolerated while retaining progress. Safety survives arbitrary delay; liveness needs eventual connectivity and a stable leader long enough to communicate.

I distinguish proposed, replicated, committed, and applied entries. Linearizable reads need current-term commit knowledge, then current authority: ReadIndex with current-term quorum heartbeats, or a still-valid bounded-clock lease whose duration is planned strictly larger than assumed clock drift. Both paths wait until `lastApplied >= readIndex` before serving. A one-time no-op is not that gate. A higher term in a heartbeat, vote, or AppendEntries forces step-down and refused reads. Membership changes must preserve old/new quorum intersection, and external resources must reject stale fencing epochs. Consensus scopes to one group; cross-shard invariants need another protocol. See [replication and failover](/learn/replication-and-failover), [CAP and practical consistency](/learn/cap-and-consistency-models), and [idempotency](/learn/idempotency-and-deduplication).”

</InterviewAnswer>
